As of a few days ago I am no longer on any debating executive bodies. My term on the national executive ended a few weeks ago, and my term at my uni society's has also wrapped up.
I finish my masters in February, and only active students are allowed to speak.
I will still judge, but I am no longer embedded in the heart of things.
I recently realised I've spent the majority of my adult life serving the New Zealand debating community, and before I say goodbye to it all I want to get some of the impacts down on record.

First and foremost, debating has some of the most interesting, intelligent people in the country.
It requires and teaches a huge amount of knowledge about the world, and the key skill is the ability to synthesise this knowledge.
It also requires a degree of confidence, which selects for people who aren't afraid to have a personality in public.
I'm a naturally confrontational person, and in debating there are people who can meet me at that level.
I have met so many of my friends through debating, also romantic interests.
I find it interesting I have the level of support I do.
I've been told I can be blunt, and as my girlfriend says, rocking the boat is one of my favourite hobbies.
The only reason I haven't been ostracised more is because I think it's so abundantly clear I'm doing it all to make things better, particularly around disability inclusion.

Aging in debating feels like a microcosm of life (I say in my mid 20s).
I remember coming in, fresh faced and excited by everything.
The speakers were so much better than I could possible imagine, there were all these people and procedures working to ensure I had a good time, and there were so many new and interesting people to meet.
I stuck around for a few years and got involved in those processes, this was also the period of time in which I knew everyone.
It felt good to be doing things, although I needed direction.
Now, six years into uni debating, I am a senior member of the community.
It's an odd feeling to have people stop and listen when I speak, and to be able to sway people.
I have a reputation that precedes me -- I've been told I'm intimidating, but also that I've inspired people.
In my old age I see how it all fits together and I have a vision for change.
Unfortunately, now that I'm busy with life, and speaking against higher ranked teams, I don't get to know the freshers.
It feels weird looking at the kids now, seeing how young they are.
I feel like a bad person sometimes.
I remember being a fresher and getting the impression the older members didn't really see me.
Now being on the other side I see how many people come briefly but don't stick around.
Until they've come back for a second year they all seem so insubstantial.
The final step of this journey through life is in the next few years, when I come back as a graduate to judge at tournaments with so much less energy than the 18 year olds and too much maturity to make dumb (fun) decisions.
Graduates (dinos if they're old enough) often have the appearance of speaking from on high, passing down wisdom from a smarter age.
The difference in life experience between 18 and 24 is evident in these moments.

The community dynamics are fascinating.
There's an oral culture; stories are told of speakers from times gone by.
To a fresh 18 year old the stories were crazy, there was someone who wanted to get a fatwa against their soceity, someone else made a cocktail featuring the Waikato river.
Some debaters go into politics, and hearing stories about the people on TV as dumb teenagers is an eye opening moment.

The political dynamics have also fascinated me since I attended my first national AGM.
Back then I struggled to comprehend all the different personalities and perspectives, but each campus has a reputation and I could use that to simplify arguments to the point I could follow.
Auckland were sweaty keeners, Waikato were trying so hard and everyone felt a little bad, Vic (when I was there was) the cool one with the most reasonable takes (they were recovering from a reputation similar to Auckland's from the previous generation), Canterbury were trying so hard but people didn't feel as bad, and Otago were cool and successful but sometimes a little too easygoing (except when it came to Auckland).
As I've grown I've been able to handle the increased complexity of the full reality.
I do wonder if I'd have this level of social competency without those training wheels.

The thing about volunteer work is that no one really wants to do it, they're only doing it because someone has to.
That's how I became the person responsible for organising multiple national tournaments.
Looking back, I would not have chosen someone like me to lead, I had issues with clear communication and arriving on time.
I cared so much about getting them right, and the rest of my team were excellent.
I suppose I learnt project management, team leadership, and delegation, but in reality things went so smoothly I'm not sure I learnt a huge amount -- that or I'm naturally gifted.
Having served on so many different executives over so many years, I've seen a variety of different leadership styles and social cultures.
I'm not sure I learnt as much from that aspect of debating, because I have no idea how to replicate the good leaders beyond the basics of being clearly passionate and hardworking, and delegating as much as possible.

Coaching
Huge time investment
But they're so keen
Every year I'd wonder if they'd learnt much of anything, there'd be small progress but still so many mistakes, and then I'd get to Nationals and stop nitpicking (and they'd get in the zone) and every time I would be shocked at how good they were.
It was so immensely gratifying to see all of the work pay off and to see them happy.
There was one year that was a bit of a disaster though, I coached with one of my best friends.
I now realise we're friends because I find our contrasting attitudes fun; at the time I coaching would be easy because got on so well (we argued, but we're debaters so that was normal).
However she is the sort of person to schedule a day down to 15 minute increments and I am in improviser.
We discussed what to teach about, but now I know to talk about how to teach; one of those lessons that's obvious in hindsight.

Something which frustrated me as a novice was the lack of resources to learn; all I had was a minute or two of feedback each week.
Unsurprisingly, progress was slow.
While there are some resources online they are difficult to find, incomplete, and difficult to put together into a coherent understanding of debating.
In the past two years I've served as Senior Development Officer for my society, and so I took it upon myself to fix this.
I feel a little self important saying this, but I believe I have written the most comprehensive debating guide available on the internet.
As I write this article I have written 30,000 words for the https://cantydebsoc.org.nz[UC Debsoc website] on how to speak and judge, and more on the https://vicdebsoc.org.nz[Vic Debsoc website] about how debating in New Zealand runs.
I remember hating writing as a kid, turns out it was because I didn't have anything to say.
There's probably also some contribution from writing my honours and masters theses, but the routine of a bunch of small, similar articles really helped.

There are a handful of skills I've developed which I think are rarer outside debating. I'm planning to write longer articles about them when I get time, so I'll just list them for now. *Critical thinking* is something people talk about it a lot, but I don't get the impression many people understand it properly, which is fair because it's difficult. Similarly, people think they understand *how to listen* but it's only I started practicing listening that I realised how much I'd been missing. *Active following* is a skill I believe to be more important than leadership, but isn't taught (perhaps for political reasons). And finally, perhaps most obviously, *communication*.

---

There's something about debating that inspires people to put in hours of volunteer time. This past year I've spent 10-15 hours a week, on top of my masters and my job. I think everyone involved knows how special this small world is and will do what it takes to keep it alive. I felt like I had to put in the work to see debating flourish because no one else would. Now that I've stepped back and the hustle is bustling without me I keep waiting for everything to fall apart, but of course it doesn't. It is gratifying to see my ideas taken up by new members, of course, but I'm left wondering what to do. If debating is life, I suppose this is retirement.

As a speaker the highest I placed was 19th in New Zealand. I am pleased with that, it's a great position and it was the result of a lot of hard work and growth. But I wanted 1st. It's frustrating, in the past few months I feel I've finally seen to the heart of debating, just in time to give it up. It's bittersw
